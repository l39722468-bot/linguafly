## Parte I — Teoría

### 1. Repaso del sistema condicional básico

Antes de abordar los condicionales avanzados conviene fijar el sistema base. El condicional cero (zero conditional) expresa verdades generales: *if you heat water to 100 degrees, it boils*. El primer condicional (first conditional) maneja situaciones reales futuras: *if it rains tomorrow, I will stay home*. El segundo condicional (second conditional) trata hipótesis presentes o futuras irreales: *if I had time, I would travel*. El tercer condicional (third conditional) se ocupa del pasado irreal: *if I had studied, I would have passed*. Sobre estos cuatro pilares se construyen los patrones avanzados que distinguen al usuario C1.

A2: "If" sentences have two parts: the "if" part and the result part.

B2: There are four basic conditional types: zero (general truths), first (real future), second (hypothetical present/future) and third (unreal past). Each has its own tense pattern.

C1: The base conditional system comprises four types: the zero (general truths, present + present), the first (real future, present + will), the second (hypothetical present/future, past + would) and the third (unreal past, past perfect + would have). Upon these four pillars the advanced patterns are constructed: mixed conditionals, inversion, and formal lexical alternatives to if. The C1 user internalises the base system as prerequisite and then extends it into the territory of temporal mixture and syntactic inversion.

### 2. Condicionales mixtos: condición pasada, resultado presente

El condicional mixto más frecuente combina una condición pasada no realizada con un resultado presente: *if I had studied medicine, I would be a doctor now*. La condición está en past perfect (*had studied*) y el resultado en conditional simple (*would be*), porque la consecuencia de aquella decisión no tomada se extiende hasta el presente. Este patrón exige pensar en qué tiempo pertenece cada parte de la hipótesis, no aplicar mecánicamente una plantilla. El error típico del B2 es usar *would have* en el resultado cuando la consecuencia es presente: *if I had studied medicine, I would have been a doctor now* es incorrecto, porque el *now* exige *would be*.

A2: Sometimes the "if" part is about the past and the result is about now.

B2: A mixed conditional can have a past condition and a present result: "If I had studied medicine, I would be a doctor now." The condition uses past perfect; the result uses "would" + present.

C1: The most frequent mixed conditional combines an unrealised past condition with a present result: if I had studied medicine, I would be a doctor now. The condition stands in the past perfect (had studied) and the result in the conditional simple (would be), because the consequence of that untaken decision extends into the present. This pattern demands thinking about which timeframe each part of the hypothesis belongs to, not applying a template mechanically. The typical B2 error is using would have in the result when the consequence is present.

### 3. Condicionales mixtos: condición presente/general, resultado pasado

El patrón inverso combina una condición permanente o general con un resultado pasado: *if I were a better driver, I would not have crashed yesterday*. La condición (*were*) describe un estado permanente del sujeto; el resultado (*would not have crashed*) se refiere a un evento pasado. Aquí la condición usa el subjuntivo *were* (segundo condicional) y el resultado usa *would have* + participio (tercer condicional). La mezcla temporal es lógicamente coherente: una característica estable del sujeto, de haber sido distinta, habría cambiado un desenlace concreto del pasado.

A2: Sometimes the "if" part is about always and the result is about the past.

B2: Another mixed conditional has a general condition and a past result: "If I were a better driver, I would not have crashed yesterday." The condition uses "were"; the result uses "would have."

C1: The inverse pattern combines a permanent or general condition with a past result: if I were a better driver, I would not have crashed yesterday. The condition (were) describes a stable trait of the subject; the result (would not have crashed) refers to a past event. The condition uses the subjunctive were (second conditional) and the result uses would have + participle (third conditional). The temporal mixture is logically coherent: a stable characteristic of the subject, had it been different, would have altered a concrete past outcome.

### 4. Inversión con *had* en el tercer condicional

La inversión condicional consiste en suprimir *if* y anteponer el auxiliar. En el tercer condicional, *if I had known* se convierte en *had I known*. El resto de la oración no cambia: *had I known the consequences, I would have acted differently*. Esta inversión es un rasgo de registro formal y literario, frecuente en ensayos académicos y en prosa cuidada, pero ausente en la conversación cotidiana. El usuario C1 la reconoce pasivamente y la despliega activamente cuando el contexto requiere elevación de registro. El error típico es mantener *if* después de invertir: *had I known if* es redundante e incorrecto.

A2: In formal writing, you can drop "if" and put "had" first: "Had I known."

B2: In the third conditional, you can invert by dropping "if" and moving "had" to the front: "Had I known the truth, I would have told you." This sounds formal and literary.

C1: Conditional inversion consists in suppressing if and fronting the auxiliary. In the third conditional, if I had known becomes had I known; the remainder of the clause is unchanged: had I known the consequences, I would have acted differently. This inversion is a marker of formal and literary register, frequent in academic essays and careful prose but absent from everyday conversation. The C1 user recognises it passively and deploys it actively when the context demands register elevation. The typical error is retaining if after inverting: had I known if is redundant and incorrect.

### 5. Inversión con *should* en el primer condicional

La inversión con *should* aparece en condicionales de futuro hipotético o improbable: *if you should need help, call me* se convierte en *should you need help, call me*. El *should* aquí no indica obligación sino probabilidad remota —equivale a "in the unlikely event that"—. Es una fórmula de cortesía formal muy frecuente en correspondencia comercial y en instrucciones administrativas: *should you require further information, do not hesitate to contact us*. El usuario C1 la domina como chunk fijo y la reconoce como marca de registro institucional.

A2: In formal texts, you can say "should you need help" instead of "if you need help."

B2: Inversion with "should" is used for unlikely future situations: "Should you need help, call me." It means "in the unlikely event that you need help." Common in business letters.

C1: Inversion with should appears in hypothetical or improbable future conditionals: if you should need help, call me becomes should you need help, call me. The should here does not indicate obligation but remote probability —equivalent to "in the unlikely event that"—. It is a formal courtesy formula very frequent in business correspondence and administrative instructions: should you require further information, do not hesitate to contact us. The C1 user masters it as a fixed chunk and recognises it as a marker of institutional register.

### 6. Inversión con *were* en el segundo condicional

La inversión con *were* se da en condicionales hipotéticos: *if I were to accept the offer, I would move to London* se convierte en *were I to accept the offer, I would move to London*. La construcción *were to* marca una hipótesis futura remota o formal, más distante que un segundo condicional simple. Es frecuente en el discurso periodístico y académico para formular escenarios contrafactuales con prudencia: *were the policy to be reversed, the consequences would be severe*. El usuario C1 distingue *were I to* (hipótesis formal futura) de *if I were* (hipótesis general) y elige cada uno según el grado de formalidad y de remota probabilidad que quiere transmitir.

A2: You can say "were I to go" instead of "if I went" in very formal writing.

B2: Inversion with "were" is used in hypothetical conditionals: "Were I to accept the offer, I would move." The phrase "were to" makes the hypothesis sound more remote and formal.

C1: Inversion with were occurs in hypothetical conditionals: if I were to accept the offer, I would move to London becomes were I to accept the offer, I would move to London. The construction were to marks a remote or formal future hypothesis, more distant than a plain second conditional. It is frequent in journalistic and academic discourse for formulating counterfactual scenarios with prudence: were the policy to be reversed, the consequences would be severe. The C1 user distinguishes were I to (formal future hypothesis) from if I were (general hypothesis) and selects each according to the degree of formality and remote probability to be conveyed.

### 7. *Unless* como alternativa restrictiva a *if*

*Unless* significa "except if" o "if not" y se usa cuando la excepción es la única condición que impediría el resultado: *I will go to the party unless I have to work*. Su restricción de uso es importante: no aparece en condicionales hipotéticos irreales. No se dice *I would help you unless I were busy*; se dice *I would help you if I were not busy*. *Unless* requiere que la condición sea real y futura. El error típico del B2 es tratar *unless* como sinónimo perfecto de *if not* en todos los contextos, cuando en realidad *unless* aporta un matiz de exclusión que *if not* no tiene.

A2: "Unless" means "if not." "I will go unless it rains" means "I will go if it doesn't rain."

B2: "Unless" means "except if." Use it when the exception is the only thing that would stop the result. Don't use "unless" in unreal hypothetical sentences.

C1: Unless signifies "except if" or "if not" and is used when the exception is the sole condition that would prevent the result: I will go to the party unless I have to work. Its usage restriction is significant: it does not appear in unreal hypothetical conditionals. One does not say I would help you unless I were busy; one says I would help you if I were not busy. Unless requires the condition to be real and future. The typical B2 error is treating unless as a perfect synonym of if not in all contexts, when in fact unless carries an exclusivity nuance that if not lacks.

### 8. *Provided that* y *as long as*: condiciones restrictivas

*Provided that* y *as long as* introducen condiciones que el hablante presenta como necesarias y suficientes: *I will lend you the car provided that you return it by Friday*. La diferencia entre ambas es de registro: *provided that* es más formal y escrito; *as long as* es más coloquial pero perfectamente aceptable en registros cuidados. *On condition that* es aún más formal y jurídico. Estas alternativas a *if* permiten precisar que la condición no es meramente posible sino exigida: el resultado no se producirá si la condición no se cumple. El usuario C1 las despliega para modular la fuerza de la condición.

A2: "As long as" means "if." "You can play as long as you finish your homework."

B2: "Provided that" and "as long as" introduce a necessary condition: "I will lend you the car provided that you return it by Friday." "Provided that" is more formal than "as long as."

C1: Provided that and as long as introduce conditions that the speaker presents as necessary and sufficient: I will lend you the car provided that you return it by Friday. The difference between them is one of register: provided that is more formal and written; as long as is more colloquial yet perfectly acceptable in careful registers. On condition that is yet more formal and juridical. These alternatives to if allow the writer to specify that the condition is not merely possible but required: the result will not obtain if the condition is not met. The C1 user deploys them to modulate the force of the condition.

### 9. *Supposing that* y *assuming that*: condiciones exploratorias

*Supposing that* y *assuming that* introducen condiciones de carácter exploratorio o hipotético, en las que el hablante invita al interlocutor a considerar un escenario: *supposing that the funding were cut, how would the project survive?*. La diferencia entre ambas es sutil: *supposing that* es más conjetural y plantea un escenario abierto; *assuming that* presupone que la condición se cumple y explora sus consecuencias. Ambas son frecuentes en discusión académica y en planificación estratégica, donde se ensayan escenarios alternativos. El usuario C1 distingue el matiz conjetural del matiz presuposicional y elige la fórmula adecuada.

A2: "Supposing" means "imagine if." "Supposing it rains, what will we do?"

B2: "Supposing that" and "assuming that" introduce a hypothetical scenario: "Supposing that the funding were cut, how would the project survive?" They invite the listener to consider a possibility.

C1: Supposing that and assuming that introduce conditions of an exploratory or hypothetical character, in which the speaker invites the interlocutor to consider a scenario: supposing that the funding were cut, how would the project survive? The difference is subtle: supposing that is more conjectural and poses an open scenario; assuming that presupposes that the condition holds and explores its consequences. Both are frequent in academic discussion and strategic planning, where alternative scenarios are rehearsed. The C1 user distinguishes the conjectural nuance from the presuppositional and selects the appropriate formula.

### 10. *In case* frente a *if*

*In case* y *if* no son intercambiables. *In case* introduce una precaución: *take an umbrella in case it rains* significa "lleva un paraguas por si llueve", es decir, como precaución ante una posibilidad. *If* introduce una condición: *take an umbrella if it rains* significa "lleva un paraguas si llueve", es decir, cuando ya está lloviendo. La confusión es frecuente en B2 y produce malentendidos. El usuario C1 distingue la precaución anticipatoria (*in case*) de la condición reactiva (*if*) y nunca las confunde, porque sabe que la diferencia semántica tiene consecuencias prácticas: con *in case* actúas antes; con *if* actúas después.

A2: "In case" means "because it might." "Take an umbrella in case it rains" means take it now because it might rain.

B2: "In case" and "if" are different. "Take an umbrella in case it rains" means take it now as a precaution. "Take an umbrella if it rains" means take it when it starts raining.

C1: In case and if are not interchangeable. In case introduces a precaution: take an umbrella in case it rains means carry an umbrella as a precaution against a possibility. If introduces a condition: take an umbrella if it rains means take one when it is already raining. The confusion is frequent at B2 and produces misunderstandings. The C1 user distinguishes anticipatory precaution (in case) from reactive condition (if) and never conflates them, knowing that the semantic difference has practical consequences: with in case you act beforehand; with if you act afterwards.

### 11. *Were to* como marcador de hipótesis remota

La construcción *were to* marca una hipótesis futura presentada como remota o improbable: *if the government were to raise taxes sharply, public discontent would follow*. Compara con *if the government raised taxes sharply*: este último presenta la hipótesis como más plausible. *Were to* añade distancia psicológica y es por ello frecuente en análisis de escenarios y en discurso condicional formal. Combinado con inversión, produce *were the government to raise taxes sharply*, la forma más distante y formal de todas. El usuario C1 calibra la distancia hipotética con *were to* del mismo modo que calibra la prudencia epistémica con hedges.

A2: "Were to" makes a future idea sound less likely.

B2: "If the government were to raise taxes" sounds more unlikely than "if the government raised taxes." Use "were to" for distant or improbable future scenarios.

C1: The construction were to marks a future hypothesis presented as remote or improbable: if the government were to raise taxes sharply, public discontent would follow. Compare with if the government raised taxes sharply: the latter presents the hypothesis as more plausible. Were to adds psychological distance and is therefore frequent in scenario analysis and formal conditional discourse. Combined with inversion, it produces were the government to raise taxes sharply, the most distant and formal form of all. The C1 user calibrates hypothetical distance with were to as they calibrate epistemic prudence with hedges.

### 12. Condicionales omitidos y elididos

En inglés cuidado, la cláusula condicional puede omitirse cuando el contexto la recupera: *I would have helped, but I did not know*. Aquí la condición (*if I had known*) está elidida y solo queda el resultado (*I would have helped*). Esta elipsis es frecuente en respuesta a preguntas: "Would you travel if you had time?" — "I would." El usuario C1 maneja la elipsis condicional con naturalidad, tanto en producción como en comprensión, y reconoce que la omisión no es incorrecta sino económicamente elegante cuando el contexto suministra la condición omitida.

A2: Sometimes we don't say the "if" part because people know it. "I would, but I can't."

B2: You can leave out the "if" clause when the context makes it clear: "I would have helped, but I didn't know" means "if I had known, I would have helped."

C1: In careful English, the conditional clause may be omitted when the context recovers it: I would have helped, but I did not know. Here the condition (if I had known) is elided and only the result (I would have helped) remains. This ellipsis is frequent in answers to questions: "Would you travel if you had time?" — "I would." The C1 user handles conditional ellipsis naturally, in both production and comprehension, and recognises that the omission is not incorrect but economically elegant when the context supplies the missing condition.

### 13. Condicionales con *but for* y *without*

*But for* y *without* introducen condiciones negativas de manera compacta: *but for your help, I would have failed* equivale a *if it had not been for your help, I would have failed*. *Without* funciona de modo similar: *without funding, the project would collapse*. Ambas son fórmulas formales que condensan un condicional negativo en una preposición o locución prepositiva. El usuario C1 las prefiere cuando quiere economía sintáctica y registro elevado, y reconoce que *but for* es más literario que *without*. La distinción de matiz le permite graduar la formalidad sin cambiar la estructura condicional.

A2: "But for" means "without." "But for your help, I would have failed" means without your help, I would have failed.

B2: "But for" and "without" introduce a negative condition compactly: "But for your help, I would have failed" means "if you hadn't helped, I would have failed." They are formal and concise.

C1: But for and without introduce negative conditions compactly: but for your help, I would have failed is equivalent to if it had not been for your help, I would have failed. Without functions similarly: without funding, the project would collapse. Both are formal formulas that condense a negative conditional into a preposition or prepositional phrase. The C1 user prefers them when syntactic economy and elevated register are desired, and recognises that but for is more literary than without. The nuance distinction allows calibrating formality without altering the conditional structure.

### 14. Inversión con *will* y *would*: registro hipotético enfatizado

En el inglés hablado enfático y en la prosa periodística aparece una inversión con *will* o *would* que no es condicional sino desiderativa o conjetural: *that will be the postman*, *that would be the bus*. No confundir con la inversión condicional. La inversión condicional siempre suprime *if* y antepone un auxiliar de tiempo (*had*, *should*, *were*). La inversión con *will/would* antepone el modal para expresar probabilidad o deseo y no deriva de un condicional. El usuario C1 distingue ambas inversiones y no las confunde, porque sabe que su función retórica y su origen sintáctico son distintos.

A2: Sometimes we put "will" or "would" at the front for emphasis.

B2: Inversion with "will" or "would" can express probability: "That will be the postman." This is not a conditional inversion; it is just emphasis or guessing.

C1: In emphatic spoken English and journalistic prose an inversion with will or would appears that is not conditional but desiderative or conjectural: that will be the postman, that would be the bus. Do not confuse it with conditional inversion. Conditional inversion always suppresses if and fronts a temporal auxiliary (had, should, were). Inversion with will/would fronts the modal to express probability or desire and does not derive from a conditional. The C1 user distinguishes both inversions and never conflates them, knowing that their rhetorical function and syntactic origin differ.

### 15. Errores frecuentes y cómo evitarlos

Los errores más frecuentes en condicionales avanzados son cuatro. Primero, mezclar tiempos en condicionales mixtos sin lógica temporal (*if I had studied, I would be a doctor yesterday*). Segundo, mantener *if* tras invertir (*had I known if*). Tercero, usar *would* en ambas cláusulas (*if I would have known, I would have acted*), un error muy común en hispanohablantes. Cuarto, confundir *unless* con *if not* en contextos hipotéticos irreales. El usuario C1 audita su producción condicional contra estos cuatro errores y los elimina sistemáticamente, porque sabe que cada uno delata un nivel intermedio no superado.

A2: Some common mistakes are using "would" twice or mixing up times.

B2: Common errors in advanced conditionals: mixing tenses without logic, keeping "if" after inverting, using "would" in both clauses, and confusing "unless" with "if not."

C1: The most frequent errors in advanced conditionals are four. First, mixing tenses in mixed conditionals without temporal logic (if I had studied, I would be a doctor yesterday). Second, retaining if after inverting (had I known if). Third, using would in both clauses (if I would have known, I would have acted), an error very common among Spanish speakers. Fourth, confusing unless with if not in unreal hypothetical contexts. The C1 user audits their conditional production against these four errors and eliminates them systematically, knowing that each betrays an untranscended intermediate level.

## Parte II — Ejercicios

**Ejercicio 1.** Completa el condicional mixto: "If I had accepted the job offer, I ____ in Madrid now."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*would be*. *If I had accepted the job offer, I would be in Madrid now.* Condición pasada, resultado presente: *would be*, no *would have been*.
</details>

**Ejercicio 2.** Invierte el siguiente tercer condicional: "If I had known the truth, I would have acted differently."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*Had I known the truth, I would have acted differently.* Se suprime *if* y se antepone *had*.
</details>

**Ejercicio 3.** Reescribe con inversión con *should*: "If you should need assistance, please call reception."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*Should you need assistance, please call reception.* Fórmula de cortesía formal frecuente en contextos institucionales.
</details>

**Ejercicio 4.** Reescribe con inversión con *were*: "If the policy were to be reversed, the consequences would be severe."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*Were the policy to be reversed, the consequences would be severe.* Inversión con *were to* en condicional hipotético formal.
</details>

**Ejercicio 5.** Completa el condicional mixto inverso: "If she were more organised, she ____ the deadline last week."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*would have met*. *If she were more organised, she would have met the deadline last week.* Condición permanente (segundo condicional), resultado pasado (tercer condicional).
</details>

**Ejercicio 6.** Corrige el error: "If I would have studied harder, I would have passed the exam."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*If I **had** studied harder, I would have passed the exam.* No se usa *would* en la cláusula de condición. Error típicamente hispanohablante.
</details>

**Ejercicio 7.** Reescribe con *unless*: "I will attend the meeting if I am not ill."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*I will attend the meeting unless I am ill.* *Unless* introduce la excepción que impediría el resultado.
</details>

**Ejercicio 8.** Explica por qué es incorrecto: "I would help you unless I were busy."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*Unless* no se usa en condicionales hipotéticos irreales. Corrección: *I would help you if I were not busy.* *Unless* requiere condiciones reales y futuras.
</details>

**Ejercicio 9.** Reescribe con *provided that*: "I will sign the contract if you guarantee delivery by March."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*I will sign the contract provided that you guarantee delivery by March.* *Provided that* presenta la condición como necesaria y formal.
</details>

**Ejercicio 10.** Reescribe con *but for*: "If it had not been for your intervention, the deal would have fallen through."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*But for your intervention, the deal would have fallen through.* *But for* condensa el condicional negativo en una locución prepositiva formal.
</details>

**Ejercicio 11.** Distingue *in case* de *if*: "Take a jacket ____ it gets cold tonight" (precaución anticipatoria).
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*in case*. *Take a jacket in case it gets cold tonight.* La precaución se toma antes de que ocurra el evento; *if* implicaría actuar cuando ya hace frío.
</details>

**Ejercicio 12.** Completa con *were to*: "If the company ____ expand into Asia, it would need significant capital."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*were to*. *If the company were to expand into Asia, it would need significant capital.* *Were to* marca hipótesis futura remota y formal.
</details>

**Ejercicio 13.** Reescribe con *supposing that*: "Imagine the candidate won the election; what would change?"
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*Supposing that the candidate won the election, what would change?* *Supposing that* invita a considerar un escenario conjetural abierto.
</details>

**Ejercicio 14.** Identifica y corrige el error en: "Had I known if, I would have told you."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

El *if* es redundante tras la inversión. Corrección: *Had I known, I would have told you.* La inversión ya sustituye a *if*; mantenerlo es incorrecto.
</details>

**Ejercicio 15.** Reescribe con *on condition that*: "I will co-author the paper if you include me in the acknowledgements."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*I will co-author the paper on condition that you include me in the acknowledgements.* *On condition that* es la alternativa más formal y jurídica a *if*.
</details>

**Ejercicio 16.** Completa el condicional mixto: "If he were a more patient negotiator, he ____ the contract yesterday."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*would have secured*. *If he were a more patient negotiator, he would have secured the contract yesterday.* Condición permanente + resultado pasado.
</details>

**Ejercicio 17.** Reescribe con *without*: "If there were no public transport, the city would grind to a halt."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*Without public transport, the city would grind to a halt.* *Without* condensa el condicional negativo de modo económico.
</details>

**Ejercicio 18.** Invierte con *were to*: "If interest rates were to rise sharply, the housing market would cool."
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*Were interest rates to rise sharply, the housing market would cool.* Inversión con *were to* para hipótesis formal remota.
</details>

**Ejercicio 19.** Redacta una oración que combine un condicional mixto y una inversión, sobre el tema de la política climática.
<details>
<summary>Ver solución</summary>

*Had governments acted decisively a decade ago, the current trajectory of emissions would be markedly less steep.* Tercer condicional invertido en la condición (pasado) y condicional simple en el resultado (presente): condicional mixto con inversión.
</details>

## Resumen de la unidad

En esta unidad hemos recorrido los condicionales avanzados del inglés C1. Hemos repasado el sistema base de cuatro tipos como cimiento indispensable. Hemos trabajado los condicionales mixtos en sus dos direcciones: condición pasada con resultado presente y condición permanente con resultado pasado. Hemos abordado la inversión condicional con *had*, *should* y *were*, distinguiendo su función de registro formal y literario. Hemos estudiado alternativas léxicas a *if*: *unless* (restrictiva), *provided that* y *as long as* (condición necesaria), *supposing that* y *assuming that* (exploratoria), *but for* y *without* (condición negativa compacta), *were to* (hipótesis remota). Hemos distinguido *in case* de *if* y la inversión condicional de la inversión con *will/would*. Y hemos catalogado los cuatro errores más frecuentes que delatan un nivel intermedio no superado. El usuario C1 no solo aplica condicionales correctos: los calibra según el registro, la distancia hipotética y la mezcla temporal que cada contexto exige. En la siguiente unidad abordaremos el subjuntivo mandativo, donde la sintaxis formal converge con el registro jurídico y administrativo.
