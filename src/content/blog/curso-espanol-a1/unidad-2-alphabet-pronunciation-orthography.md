English readers often approach Spanish pronunciation with an English alphabet and English vowel habits. That feels sensible, but it creates two predictable problems: one letter can represent several English sounds, and an English vowel can glide when a Spanish learner needs it to stay still. Spanish gives you a more regular map. Learn the map and you can read a new name, ask someone to repeat a spelling, and make yourself understood before you know many verbs.

This is **Unit 2 of the A1 Spanish course** (*Alphabet, pronunciation, and orthography*). By the end, you will pronounce the five vowels, recognise **ñ, j, rr, and ll**, use a written accent as a stress clue, spell your name, and write a complete question with **¿ ?**.

## What you will be able to do

After this unit you can:

- Say the five vowels with a consistent Spanish target instead of an English glide.
- Name the Spanish letters and ask **¿Cómo se escribe?** (“How is it spelled?”).
- Spell a first name slowly, including **ñ** and a written accent.
- Hear and produce useful contrasts such as **pero** (“but”) and **perro** (“dog”).
- Place **¿** before and **?** after a written question.
- Use a written accent to locate stress in words such as **teléfono**, **inglés**, and **María**.
- Read a short A1 exchange aloud, stopping at punctuation rather than guessing from English rhythm.

The goal is intelligibility. Spanish has regional accents, and **ll** may sound like English *y* or a soft *j* depending on the speaker. Do not wait for a perfect trill before speaking.

## Grammar and orthography: the punctuation of a question

Unit 1 gave you **¿Cómo te llamas?** as a useful chunk. Now notice its written architecture. Spanish opens a question with **¿** and closes it with **?**:

| Function | Spanish | English |
|---|---|---|
| Ask a name | ¿Cómo te llamas? | What is your name? |
| Ask for spelling | ¿Cómo se escribe? | How is it spelled? |
| Ask about a letter | ¿Con b o con v? | With b or with v? |
| Check a sound | ¿Perro o pero? | Dog or but? |
| Ask for repetition | ¿Puedes repetir? | Can you repeat? |

The inverted mark is a reading signal. It tells you to begin question intonation at that point, not only when you reach the final word. In a long sentence, this is especially helpful: **Después de la clase, ¿cómo se escribe tu apellido?** (“After class, how is your surname spelled?”). In informal digital messages, people sometimes omit **¿**, but careful Spanish keeps both marks. Do not copy the casual omission into a form, email, or learning exercise.

Spanish does not require a change in word order for every question. **¿Tú eres Ana?** and **¿Eres Ana?** are both possible. The punctuation and rising intonation make the question clear. English speakers often add an unnecessary auxiliary: *¿Do you are Ana?* That is not Spanish. Use the vocabulary you know and let **¿ ?** frame it.

## The Spanish alphabet

Letter names are useful when somebody asks you to spell a surname. Names vary a little by country, so the table gives widely understood forms and an English-friendly approximation. The approximation is a starting point, not a new English spelling.

| K | ka | kah |

In Spain, **c** before *e/i* and **z** often have the sound of *th* in *thin*: **cero**, **zapato**. In most of Latin America they sound like *s*. Both patterns are standard. **B** and **v** are frequently pronounced alike in ordinary speech, so spelling questions matter: **b de burro** (“b as in donkey”) and **v de vaca** (“v as in cow”) can prevent confusion.

## The five vowels: one letter, one stable target

English has tense and lax vowels, silent letters, and diphthongs. Spanish vowels are shorter and more predictable:

| Vowel | Example | Careful target | English-speaker reminder |
|---|---|---|---|
| **a** | **casa** | open “ah” | Do not finish with an *uh* glide. |
| **e** | **mesa** | clear “eh” | Keep it away from the *ay* in *name*. |
| **i** | **vino** | “ee” | Similar to *machine*, not the *i* in *sit*. |
| **o** | **como** | pure rounded “oh” | Do not turn it into the *ow* in *go*. |
| **u** | **luna** | “oo” | Do not add a *y* sound before it. |

Say **a-e-i-o-u** as five even beats. Then say **pa-pe-pi-po-pu** and **ma-me-mi-mo-mu**. Your jaw and lips should make small, controlled movements. English learners often over-stress one vowel and reduce the others to a weak *uh*. That reduction makes **pero** sound like an English unstressed word and hides the difference from **perro**.

Vowels can sit together without automatically becoming an English-style long vowel. In **María**, the accent on **í** tells you to separate the vowels: ma-RÍ-a. In **país**, say pa-ÍS, not one blended syllable. We will return to this after normal stress rules.

## Four high-value consonant spellings

### Ñ: a separate letter

**Ñ** is not an ornament over *n*. It is a separate letter, pronounced like the *ny* in *canyon*: **España**, **niño**, **año**. Hold the first part lightly and move into a palatal nasal sound. Write the tilde every time; **ano** is a different and potentially embarrassing word from **año**. The letter name is **eñe**.

### J: breath from the throat

Spanish **j** is a strong, unvoiced breath: **jamón**, **Javier**, **rojo**. There is no exact English equivalent. The sound in Scottish *loch* is a useful comparison, but make it gentle at first. English speakers may replace it with a hard *j* as in *job*; that changes the word. **José** begins with breath, not the English sound in *Joe*.

### RR and single R

At the start of a word, **r** is strong: **rojo**, **rápido**. Between vowels, single **r** is a quick tap: **pero** (“but”). Written **rr** between vowels signals a trill: **perro** (“dog”). A trill uses the tongue tip near the ridge behind your upper teeth; air makes it flutter. If you cannot trill yet, use a clear tap and keep practising. Do not replace the contrast with an English *r*, whose tongue position is different and does not tap.

| Spelling | Example | Meaning | Learning cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **r** between vowels | **pero** | but | one quick tap |
| **rr** | **perro** | dog | sustained tongue vibration |
| word-initial **r** | **rosa** | rose / pink | strong onset |

### LL: regional variation

In much of the Spanish-speaking world, **ll** in **llama**, **llave**, and **lluvia** sounds like English *y*. In areas with *yeísmo*, **ll** and **y** are pronounced alike. Some speakers use a soft “zh” sound. Learn the spelling and aim for a smooth palatal sound; do not pronounce **ll** as two separate English *l*s.

## Stress and the written accent mark

Every Spanish word with two or more syllables has a stressed syllable. Stress means the syllable is slightly clearer, longer, or stronger; it does not mean shouting. The default rules are:

1. If a word ends in a vowel, **n**, or **s**, stress the penultimate syllable: **ca-sa**, **ha-blan**, **me-sas**.
2. If a word ends in another consonant, stress the final syllable: ho-tel, es-pa-ñol, co-mer.
3. A written acute accent overrides those rules: te-lé-fo-no, in-glés, Ma-rí-a.

The mark leans upward: **á é í ó ú**. It is not an apostrophe, and it does not mean “make the vowel English.” Compare:

- **termino** = I finish (TER-mi-no)
- **terminó** = he/she finished (ter-mi-NÓ)
- **papa** = potato in many contexts
- **papá** = dad
- **si** = if
- **sí** = yes

At A1, use the accent as a practical reading instruction. When you copy a new word, underline its stressed syllable and check whether the written accent is present. In a name, preserve it: **Andrés**, **Sofía**, **García**. Do not remove it just because English keyboards make it inconvenient.

## How English and Spanish differ

The most useful correction is not “Spanish has rules”; it is seeing where an English habit fails:

- × *pay-ro* → **pero**: two pure vowels and one tap.
- × *pair-oh* → **perro**: the double **r** needs a trill.
- × *Hose-ay* → **José**: **j** is breathy, and **é** carries stress.
- × *es-PAN-ya* with an English *ny* hidden → **Es-PA-ña**: **ñ** is one consonant sound.
- × *How do you spell?* translated as *¿Cómo tú deletreas?* → **¿Cómo se escribe?** is the useful A1 chunk.
- × *Do you are Ana?* → **¿Eres Ana?** Spanish does not borrow the English auxiliary **do**.
- × *Como?* when you mean “How?” → **¿Cómo?** The accent distinguishes the question word.
- × *Me llamo Alex?* as a statement with English punctuation → **Me llamo Alex.** Use **¿Cómo te llamas?** for the question.

English capitalises days and languages; Spanish normally does not. Letter names and ordinary words are lower-case unless they begin a sentence or are proper names. Write **español**, but **España** because it is a country name.

## Model dialogue: spelling a name

**Maya:** Hola, me llamo Maya. ¿Cómo te llamas?  
**Diego:** Me llamo Diego. ¿Cómo se escribe tu nombre?  
**Maya:** M-A-Y-A: eme, a, ye, a.  
**Diego:** ¿Con i griega?  
**Maya:** Sí, con ye. ¿Y tu apellido?  
**Diego:** García: ge, a, erre, ce, í, a. La í tiene tilde.  
**Maya:** ¿García, con acento en í?  
**Diego:** Exacto.  
**Maya:** Mucho gusto, Diego.  
**Diego:** Igualmente.

**Full gloss:** Maya says hello and gives her name. Diego asks how it is written. She names each letter and confirms that **y** is **ye**. He spells **García**, explicitly mentioning the written accent on **í**. This is a realistic repair strategy: ask, repeat, and confirm instead of pretending you heard a spelling.

Check yourself: (1) Which letter does Maya clarify? (2) Which syllable has the accent in García? (3) Which punctuation frames each question?

## English-speaker pitfalls

1. **Reducing every vowel to “uh.”** × *muh-suh* → **mesa**, with two clear **e/a** targets.
2. **Making j sound like English j.** × *Joe-sé* → **José**, with breath at the start.
3. **Ignoring ñ.** × **ano** for **año** → keep the tilde and the “ny” sound.
4. **Using an English r.** × English *r* in **pero** → make a quick tongue tap.
5. **Adding or deleting r.** × **pero** when you mean a dog → **perro** has the trill and a different meaning.
6. **Dropping the opening question mark.** × **Cómo te llamas?** → **¿Cómo te llamas?**
7. **Treating an accent as decoration.** × **GARCIA** in careful Spanish → **García**, with stress on í.
8. **Translating “spell” word for word.** × *¿Cómo deletreas?* as the first choice → **¿Cómo se escribe?** is a natural beginner formula.
9. **Capitalising Spanish unnecessarily.** × *Español* in the middle of a sentence → **español**, unless it starts the sentence.

## A 15-minute practice plan

1. **Vowel ladder (3 minutes):** say **a-e-i-o-u**, then **pa-pe-pi-po-pu**, keeping every vowel audible.
2. **Sound pairs (3 minutes):** alternate **pero–perro**, **pala–palla**, **ano–año**. Record yourself and listen for the extra consonant.
3. **Stress marking (3 minutes):** underline the strong syllable in **casa, hotel, español, teléfono, María**; circle the written accents.
4. **Spelling role-play (3 minutes):** spell your first name and surname, then ask **¿Cómo se escribe? ¿Puedes repetir?**
5. **Punctuation check (3 minutes):** write five questions with **¿ ?**, read them aloud, and answer each with a short sentence.

## FAQs

### Is Spanish pronunciation completely regular?

It is more predictable than English, not perfectly uniform. Regional choices affect **z**, **c**, **ll**, and **y**, and connected speech changes the detail. The five vowels and stress rules are excellent stable foundations. Learn one clear model, listen to several speakers, and prioritise contrasts that change meaning, such as **pero/perro**.

### Does every accent mark change pronunciation?

An acute accent always gives you a stress clue, but context can also distinguish short words (**sí/si**, **tú/tu**). Never use an accent randomly. Copy the spelling of a word as a whole and notice that accents belong to particular letters, including capital letters: **Ángela**.

### How can I practise a trill if my English has no rolled r?

Start with a relaxed tongue, a steady stream of air, and a quick tap in **pero**. Repeat **tra-tre-tri-tro-tru** and **pero-perro** without forcing your throat. A tap is acceptable while you build the trill. Clear vowels and a distinction between one and two r’s matter more than performing a dramatic roll.

### Should I learn Spain or Latin-American pronunciation?

Choose the model that matches the people you expect to speak with, but understand both common patterns. The **s** pronunciation of **z/c** is widespread in Latin America; the “th” pronunciation is standard in much of Spain. **Ll** also varies. These differences rarely stop communication, while missing an accent or confusing **r/rr** can.
