### L1 — Reading

**Text type:** Adapted scientific report (550–650 words)  
**Topic:** *The State of the Climate: Key Findings and What They Mean for the Future*

#### The State of the Climate

The climate record is not a prediction of what might happen; it is a measurement of what has already changed. Across the world, observations collected by weather stations, satellites and ocean sensors point to a warming system whose effects are becoming more visible and more unevenly distributed. The evidence does not imply that every extreme event can be attributed to climate change, nor that every projection will be realised. It does indicate that the range of plausible futures is being narrowed by physical processes that are already under way.

The clearest finding concerns global temperature. The average surface temperature of the planet has risen by roughly 1.1°C above the pre-industrial level, although the figure varies between years and regions. This may appear modest when compared with daily weather, but a global average combines the conditions of oceans, polar regions and continents. Land areas are warming faster than oceans, and the Arctic is warming several times faster than the global average, a process known as Arctic amplification. The result is not simply warmer days. It alters rainfall patterns, accelerates the melting of ice and increases the likelihood of dangerous heat in places where infrastructure and public health systems are least prepared.

The second key finding is that the atmosphere contains substantially more carbon dioxide than it did before large-scale industrialisation. Carbon dioxide, methane and other greenhouse gases trap heat that would otherwise escape into space. Emissions are not distributed equally: a relatively small number of countries and industries have produced a large share of historical emissions, while many communities facing the greatest risks have contributed comparatively little. This imbalance makes climate policy an economic and political issue as well as a scientific one. Reducing emissions is essential, but so is deciding who pays for adaptation, loss and damage, and the transition to cleaner energy.

The third finding concerns the ocean. Having absorbed much of the additional heat in the climate system, the ocean is becoming warmer and, in many areas, more acidic. Ocean acidification occurs when seawater absorbs carbon dioxide and its chemistry changes, making it more difficult for some organisms to form shells or skeletons. Sea-level rise is driven by both the thermal expansion of warmer water and the loss of land-based ice. These processes unfold gradually, but their consequences can be abrupt for coastal communities: salt water can enter freshwater supplies, storms can reach further inland and relocation may become unavoidable.

The fourth finding is that climate risks can reinforce one another through feedback loops. When permafrost thaws, it may release carbon dioxide and methane, adding further warming pressure. When ice disappears, darker surfaces absorb more solar energy instead of reflecting it. Such mechanisms do not mean that a single tipping point will suddenly determine the fate of the planet. They mean that some changes can become self-reinforcing and more difficult to reverse. This is why the distinction between prevention and adaptation is increasingly misleading. Societies must reduce the causes of warming while preparing for impacts that can no longer be avoided.

The policy implications are serious but not hopeless. Mitigation measures, including renewable energy, energy efficiency, forest protection and lower-emission transport, can slow the rate of change. Adaptation measures can strengthen resilience through better water management, heat planning, flood protection and climate-resilient crops. Their effectiveness depends on implementation rather than announcement. A climate pledge without a credible pathway for monitoring and enforcement has limited value, while an imperfect policy that is consistently improved may produce substantial benefits.

The report therefore supports neither complacency nor fatalism. Attribution science can identify how climate change has altered the probability or intensity of particular events, but it cannot remove uncertainty from complex systems. Uncertainty should not be used as a reason for inaction; it should guide risk management. The evidence suggests that every fraction of a degree matters, that early intervention is less costly than unmanaged disruption and that fairness will determine whether climate measures remain politically durable. The future is not fixed, but the choices that shape it are becoming increasingly time-sensitive.

### L1 — Reading

**Text type:** Science article (500–600 words)  
**Topic:** *The Sixth Mass Extinction: What Biodiversity Loss Means for the Planet and for Us*

#### The Sixth Mass Extinction

When people hear the term biodiversity, they often think of a list of endangered animals. In reality, biodiversity describes the variety of life at several levels: genes within a species, species within an ecosystem and ecosystems across the planet. This variety is not ornamental. It enables pollination, soil formation, water purification and the regulation of pests and disease. The loss of biodiversity is therefore not only a conservation concern. It is a gradual weakening of the systems on which human societies depend.

Scientists generally identify five mass extinctions in the geological record. The current crisis is sometimes called the sixth because extinction rates are far above their long-term background level and are being driven largely by human activity. Habitat loss remains the most significant pressure in many regions. Forests, wetlands, grasslands and coastal environments are converted for agriculture, housing, roads and industry. Even where an entire habitat is not removed, it can be divided into isolated fragments. Populations then become smaller and less able to exchange genetic material, making them more vulnerable to disease, climate change and random events.

Other pressures reinforce habitat loss. Overfishing can remove key species from marine food webs, while pollution alters the conditions in which organisms feed and reproduce. Invasive species may displace native organisms that have no effective defence against them. Climate change adds another layer of disruption by shifting temperature and rainfall patterns faster than some species can adapt or migrate. A species may survive each pressure separately but fail when several occur at once. This interaction is one reason why simple counts of endangered species can underestimate the seriousness of the crisis.

The consequences extend well beyond wildlife. Crops depend on pollinators and on genetic diversity that can provide resistance to drought or disease. Mangrove forests and coral reefs protect coastal settlements from storms, while healthy soils retain water and support food production. The destruction of these services imposes economic costs, but the costs are not distributed equally. Communities with limited public infrastructure often depend most directly on local ecosystems and have the fewest resources with which to replace them. Biodiversity loss can thus deepen existing inequality, even when its immediate causes are connected to distant markets.

Conservationists increasingly argue that protecting isolated reserves is not enough. Effective conservation may require wildlife corridors that connect fragmented habitats, marine reserves that allow fish populations to recover and restoration projects that return ecological functions rather than simply planting trees. Indigenous and local communities should be involved in these decisions, since they frequently possess detailed knowledge of seasonal change and species behaviour. Their participation is also a matter of justice: people who have protected an ecosystem should not be displaced in the name of preserving it.

There are grounds for cautious optimism. Some species have recovered after hunting was restricted, wetlands have been restored and damaged landscapes have begun to regenerate when pressure was removed. However, these examples should not be used to suggest that every loss can be reversed. A captive-breeding programme cannot recreate a destroyed food web, and rewilding will fail if the underlying causes of habitat loss remain. Conservation succeeds when it combines enforcement, long-term funding and a realistic understanding of how an ecosystem functions.

The language of a sixth mass extinction can create urgency, but it can also produce helplessness. Presenting nature as a passive victim may obscure the political choices behind land use, consumption and regulation. The most useful response is neither denial nor despair. It is to recognise that biodiversity is a form of natural resilience and that protecting it is an investment in human security. The question is not whether people can survive without every species. It is whether societies are willing to preserve enough living complexity to remain adaptable in an uncertain future.

#### After the Earthquake

At 6:42 on a Tuesday morning, the first tremor lasted less than a minute. In the coastal city of San Aurelio, that was long enough to bring down apartment facades, rupture water mains and send thousands of residents into the streets. A second, weaker shock followed before emergency services had established a command centre. By midday, the city authorities had confirmed 38 fatalities, more than 400 injuries and extensive damage to roads, schools and the central hospital. Those figures were provisional, with rescue teams still searching several collapsed buildings.

The earthquake itself was not unexpected. San Aurelio lies near a fault line, and building regulations had been strengthened after a smaller tremor fifteen years earlier. Implementation, however, had been uneven. Newer hospitals and public offices had been designed to withstand severe shaking, while many older homes had not been reinforced. The disaster therefore exposed a familiar gap between what a city knows about risk and what it is willing or able to fund before a crisis occurs.

In the first hours, the response depended on coordination as much as courage. Firefighters used trained dogs and acoustic equipment to locate people beneath concrete. Medical teams converted a sports centre into an emergency clinic, where patients were assessed according to the urgency of their injuries. Volunteers distributed water, blankets and information, although unverified reports on social media briefly caused panic about a tsunami. Officials later urged residents to rely on the emergency service's updates rather than share rumours that could send people towards unsafe areas.

Among those waiting outside the damaged Rivera district was Elena Márquez, whose family had lived there for three generations. “We knew the buildings were old,” she said, “but knowing a risk exists is different from having a safe place to go.” Her comment captures one of the less visible effects of disasters. Physical damage can be counted in buildings and roads; the loss of trust, routine and a sense of control is harder to record. Children who had slept outdoors were still startled by passing lorries two weeks later, according to counsellors working at temporary shelters.

The immediate relief effort gradually gave way to a more complicated phase. Temporary accommodation had to be heated, schools had to reopen and essential businesses needed permission to operate in partially damaged streets. Engineers inspected structures, while local organisations mapped residents who were elderly, disabled or unable to access online assistance. This information mattered because a universal recovery plan could easily overlook people whose needs were not visible from the main roads.

Rebuilding has also raised a difficult question: should the city restore what existed or use the disaster to change it? Replacing unsafe buildings with stronger ones is essential, but relocating low-income residents to the outskirts could separate them from jobs, schools and family networks. Urban planners have proposed a compromise involving subsidised retrofitting, protected evacuation routes and small public spaces that can serve as assembly points. Whether these proposals survive beyond the news cycle will depend on sustained funding and public scrutiny.

San Aurelio's experience offers no simple lesson. Early-warning systems can save lives, but they cannot prevent poorly maintained buildings from failing. Emergency services can mobilise resources rapidly, but they cannot substitute for years of preparation. The city will eventually repair its roads and reopen its hospital. The more important test will be whether the reconstruction reduces vulnerability rather than merely restoring appearances. As one rescue coordinator put it, “Recovery is not the moment when the cranes leave. It is when people can make plans again.”

**Text type:** Feature article (500–600 words)  
**Topic:** *The Circular Economy: Can We Design a World Without Waste?*

#### The Circular Economy

The modern economy is still largely organised around a simple sequence: extract materials, manufacture products, use them and throw them away. This linear model has delivered remarkable convenience, but it depends on continuous access to finite resources and produces waste at every stage. A circular economy proposes a different principle. Products and materials should remain useful for as long as possible, while waste should be treated as a design failure or a potential resource rather than an inevitable by-product of prosperity.

The distinction is more substantial than replacing plastic bags or encouraging household recycling. In a circular system, products would be designed to be durable, repairable and adaptable. A phone whose battery can be replaced is more circular than one sealed inside a case that is difficult to open. Furniture could be leased, repaired and eventually disassembled so that its components could be used again. Manufacturers would have an incentive to retain ownership of materials, because their future value would remain part of the business model. The aim would be to reduce the need for new extraction before trying to manage discarded goods.

Cities are a particularly important testing ground. They concentrate consumers, infrastructure and waste, but they also bring together the organisations needed to share resources. Construction materials recovered from demolished buildings can be catalogued and reused in new projects. Organic waste can be turned into compost or biogas. District heating systems may capture energy that would otherwise be lost. Such measures can reduce emissions and create local employment, although their success depends on reliable collection systems and planning rules that allow materials to circulate.

Digital technology is often presented as an enabler of this transition. A product passport, attached to an item or its components, could record the materials used, the repairs carried out and the safest method of recycling it. Data platforms might match surplus food, unused equipment or spare industrial capacity with organisations that need them. However, efficiency alone does not guarantee sustainability. If digital systems make it cheaper to order and replace goods, they may accelerate consumption rather than reduce it. The environmental benefit depends on what behaviour the technology encourages.

This is why the circular economy cannot be reduced to individual responsibility. Consumers can repair, share and buy less, but they cannot redesign packaging or control the lifespan of appliances on their own. Regulation may be needed to require spare parts, limit planned obsolescence and make producers responsible for the goods they place on the market. Public procurement can also create demand for durable products by rewarding life-cycle value rather than the lowest immediate price. These interventions may initially increase costs, yet the apparent cheapness of disposable goods often excludes pollution, resource depletion and public health damage from the calculation.

Critics point out that circularity has physical limits. Recycling generally requires energy, and materials can degrade after repeated processing. A fully closed loop is rarely achievable: some substances are dispersed, contaminated or too complex to recover economically. There is also a risk that companies use the language of circularity to make modest improvements appear transformative. A take-back scheme is useful, but it does not compensate for a business model based on selling ever larger quantities of short-lived products.

The most credible vision is therefore neither a perfect world without waste nor a rejection of innovation. It is an economy in which waste is reduced at source, products last longer and the true costs of production are made visible. Individual choices matter, particularly when they support better systems, but structural change matters more. A circular city will not be created by asking people to sort their rubbish more carefully while buildings, regulations and supply chains remain linear. It will emerge when durability, repair and reuse become ordinary economic advantages rather than acts of personal sacrifice.

### L1 — Reading

**Text type:** Philosophical essay (550–650 words)  
**Topic:** *Nature and the Human Soul: Why Our Disconnection from the Natural World Matters*

#### Nature and the Human Soul

To say that modern people are disconnected from nature is not to claim that they have left the natural world. No one has. Every breath depends on a biological system, every meal on soil, water and other organisms, and every city on materials extracted from the earth. The disconnection is more subtle: many people experience nature as scenery, recreation or a service provider rather than as the living context that makes their lives possible. The problem is not simply that trees and rivers have become less visible. It is that the human imagination has begun to treat them as external to human concerns.

For much of history, the boundary between society and nature was difficult to maintain. Weather determined harvests, darkness limited activity and local landscapes shaped work, stories and ceremonies. Industrialisation changed this relationship by making energy, food and goods appear available on demand. Urban life did not eliminate dependence on nature; it concealed it behind pipes, roads, markets and electrical networks. A supermarket can make a seasonless abundance seem normal, while a tap can hide the watershed on which a city relies. The more successful the infrastructure, the easier it becomes to forget what supports it.

This forgetting has practical consequences. A forest is described as timber, a wetland as developable land and a river as a source of water or electricity. These descriptions are not entirely false, but they are incomplete. They reduce a complex community of living relationships to a single function and encourage decisions based on immediate utility. What disappears from view is the web of dependence: insects pollinating crops, fungi supporting trees, wetlands absorbing floods and microorganisms maintaining soil. Once these relationships are treated as background, their loss may be noticed only when a system begins to fail.

There is also a psychological cost. Many studies suggest that contact with green spaces can reduce stress, support attention and improve mood, although such findings should not be turned into a simplistic promise that a walk can cure every difficulty. The deeper value of nature may lie in the way it changes the scale of human concerns. A person watching a tide or following the growth of a tree encounters time that is not organised around deadlines and notifications. The experience can be humbling, but humility is not humiliation. It is the recognition that a meaningful life need not depend on controlling everything around it.

This idea is sometimes expressed through the metaphor of nature as a mirror. In a landscape, people encounter their own values: their desire for order, their fear of uncertainty or their willingness to share space with forms of life they cannot use. The mirror is imperfect, of course. Nature does not provide direct moral instructions, and romanticising wilderness can ignore the dangers and inequalities that exist within human communities. Nevertheless, the image reminds us that environmental decisions are also decisions about what kinds of relationships we consider worthy of protection.

Reconnecting with nature does not require abandoning cities or pretending that technology is the enemy. Urban parks, community gardens, restored waterways and tree-lined streets can make ecological processes visible again. Schools can treat local habitats as places of knowledge rather than decorative surroundings. Businesses can measure success by long-term resilience as well as short-term growth. These changes may seem modest, yet they challenge the assumption that nature begins where the built environment ends.

The ethical task is not to return to an imagined past in which humans lived in perfect harmony with the earth. Such a past never existed. It is to develop a form of belonging that accepts both human creativity and human dependence. As I see it, the natural world does not need to be made sacred before it deserves care; its vulnerability and its importance are sufficient reasons. What is remarkable is not that people can dominate a landscape for a time, but that a species capable of understanding its dependence might choose to live accordingly.

### L1 — Reading

**Text type:** Long academic/journalistic text (700+ words)  
**Topic:** *After Nature: Can Technology Save What Human Progress Has Destroyed?*

#### After Nature

The phrase “saving nature” is increasingly used to describe technologies that monitor ecosystems, restore damaged habitats or remove pollution from the air and water. It reflects a genuine shift in capability. Satellites can identify illegal deforestation almost in real time, sensors can detect changes in soil moisture and genetic tools may help populations resist emerging diseases. Yet the phrase also contains a warning. If technology is treated as a substitute for restraint, it may allow societies to continue damaging ecosystems while assuming that innovation will repair the consequences later.

The first argument in favour of technological intervention is practical necessity. Human activity has altered so many landscapes that returning them to a pre-industrial condition is often impossible. Coastal wetlands have been drained, rivers have been redirected and species have been transported across continents. In such places, conservation cannot mean simply leaving nature alone, because the conditions that once supported it no longer exist. Artificial reefs can provide surfaces for marine organisms, while carefully managed water flows can restore part of a river's ecological function. These interventions do not recreate an untouched past, but they may prevent further collapse and preserve services on which communities depend.

Technology can also make environmental change more visible. Remote sensing allows researchers to map habitat loss over large areas, while acoustic monitoring can reveal the presence of species that are difficult to observe directly. Open data platforms can connect local observations with international research and help authorities target limited resources. Such systems are particularly valuable when enforcement is weak: a protected area that exists only on paper can be compared with satellite evidence of what is happening on the ground. In this sense, technology strengthens accountability by making destruction harder to conceal.

The second argument concerns scale. Climate change, biodiversity loss and pollution operate across borders, whereas conservation projects are often small and local. A network of sensors may detect the spread of an invasive species before it reaches a new watershed. Computer models can project how a habitat might respond to changing rainfall, allowing planners to identify corridors through which animals could move. In agriculture, precision systems may reduce the use of water and fertiliser by applying them only where they are needed. None of these tools eliminates ecological risk, but each can improve the quality and timing of decisions.

The optimism becomes less convincing when technological solutions are evaluated in isolation. Carbon capture, for example, may remove emissions from industrial processes, but its cost, energy demand and storage risks remain significant. Solar geoengineering could theoretically reflect a portion of sunlight and reduce warming, yet it might alter rainfall patterns and would not solve ocean acidification. Genetic interventions could protect a vulnerable species, but releasing modified organisms into a complex ecosystem could have consequences that are difficult to reverse. The fact that a system can be manipulated does not mean that its behaviour can be predicted with confidence.

This is the third and most important argument: environmental technology is never neutral. Decisions about where to build a seawall, which species to reintroduce or whose land may be monitored distribute benefits and burdens among real communities. A rewilding project can restore an ecosystem while making traditional livelihoods harder to maintain. A conservation database can improve research while exposing the location of a species to poachers. Technical expertise is necessary, but it cannot decide alone what counts as an acceptable trade-off. Local knowledge, public consent and transparent governance are part of ecological competence, not obstacles to it.

There is also a danger of moral displacement. When a company funds a tree-planting programme, it may present the project as compensation for emissions that continue elsewhere. When a city installs smart waste bins, it may celebrate efficiency without reducing the volume of disposable packaging. These measures can be useful, but their value depends on whether they accompany changes in production and consumption. Repairing damage after it occurs is usually more expensive and less reliable than preventing it at source. No sensor can make an extinct species return, and no algorithm can restore a relationship that a community has lost with its landscape.

The most defensible position is therefore neither technological pessimism nor technological salvation. Tools should be used where they improve evidence, reduce pressure and support restoration that has a realistic ecological basis. Their deployment should be guided by the precautionary principle when effects may be irreversible, and evaluated through long-term monitoring rather than short-term publicity. Above all, innovation should operate within limits set by ecological resilience. Technology can help societies live within those limits, but it cannot abolish them.

Human progress has not placed humanity outside nature; it has increased the scale of human influence within it. The task is not to recreate an imaginary world untouched by people, but to build institutions capable of recognising dependence and acting before thresholds are crossed. As far as we know, no technological breakthrough can replace that political and ethical responsibility. The future of conservation will depend less on whether humans can control nature than on whether they can learn when not to.
